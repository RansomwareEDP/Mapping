01

Executive Summary and Group Overview

DireWolf (also styled "Dire Wolf") is a financially motivated, human-operated ransomware operation that first surfaced in May 2025, publishing its inaugural six victims to a Tor data leak site on 26 May 2025. Trustwave SpiderLabs produced the first binary analysis in June 2025, and the Cyber Security Agency of Singapore issued Alert AL-2025-082 on 18 August 2025, which remains the only government product naming the group. [1][2][3]

The group is assessed as a closed crew rather than a ransomware-as-a-service platform: an estimated three to seven operators, no observed affiliate program, victim-specific encryptor builds with hardcoded negotiation room IDs, and a single centralised Tor and Tox infrastructure. Its leak site carries a blunt statement of motive, "We only seek money. No morals, no political stance," and a claimed location of "Based in New York, NY" that every vendor reviewing it assesses as misdirection. [1][4][5]

As of 21 August 2026 the leak site carries 100 cumulative posts across 32 countries. The more analytically important figure is the shape of that activity rather than its total: after a January 2026 cluster, the site went quiet for five months, posted four victims on 12 June 2026, went quiet again for two months, then published 25 victims between 10 and 19 August 2026. DireWolf is a burst-and-dormancy operator, not a steady-tempo one, and open reporting that describes it as maintaining "sustained, undiminished" output does not survive contact with the posting record. [7][8]

Analyst Assessment: DireWolf is one of the few well-documented ransomware operations with no credible lineage to a predecessor group, no named operators, no attributed CVE, no on-chain link to another crew, and no law enforcement action of any kind after fifteen months. That combination is the profile's central finding. The group is technically competent but financially unsophisticated: TRM Labs and Halcyon both describe a cash-out path with limited obfuscation and a "potentially recoverable" structure. The leverage point here is financial, not technical, and it is currently unexploited.

02

Lineage and Organizational Heritage

Assessment: Independent Origin
Confirmed

Unusually for a 2025-era ransomware operation, DireWolf has no established lineage to any predecessor group. Halcyon states the position directly: "No identified affiliations or connections to existing ransomware groups. Represents entirely new threat actor with independent infrastructure, custom Golang codebase, and separate financial operations." No code overlap, shared infrastructure, shared affiliate, or personnel link has been published by any vendor reviewed. [4]

The assessment rests on three independent evidentiary strands rather than on absence of evidence alone.

Evidentiary Pillars
Pillar 01
On-chain separation
Halcyon reports blockchain analysis found "minimal on-chain connections through common non-custodial exchanges without shared wallets or financial links to established groups," and explicitly contrasts DireWolf against Frag (shared two-address cluster with Akira) and Kairos (shared cash-out addresses with SafePay, INC, Lynx and Qilin). TRM Labs' own profile of DireWolf describes a standalone laundering pattern and makes no cross-group linkage. [4][6]
Pillar 02
Unique code markers
The mutex Global\direwolfAppMutex and completion marker C:\runfinish.exe do not match known group signatures. The Curve25519 plus ChaCha20 per-file key exchange, the eight-goroutines-per-CPU worker pool, and the 75-service / 59-process kill lists were all observed as a coherent, finished implementation in the first sample analysed, with no version iteration since. [1][2][4]
Pillar 03
Independent infrastructure
A single Tor onion, a single published qTox ID, and per-victim negotiation rooms hosted on that same infrastructure. No shared hosting, no reused leak-site template, and no traditional command-and-control infrastructure documented by any analyst. [1][4][5]
Counter-signal
Maturity without a track record
Halcyon notes that day-one technical sophistication, zero reported bugs or failed encryptions, and polished infrastructure at launch "suggest formation by experienced operators rather than novices, possibly former affiliates from disrupted groups." This supports operator-level experience carried over from elsewhere, but it is not evidence of an organisational successor relationship and should not be read as one. [4]
The BlackCat / ALPHV Overlap Claim
Analyst Inference, single source, contradicted

Security Arsenal, a US managed-security provider, published in August 2026 that "some overlap in TTPs with former BlackCat/ALPHV affiliates is suspected but unconfirmed." The claim is flagged as unconfirmed by the source itself, cites no blockchain, code, or personnel corroboration, and rests on TTP similarity alone, which for commodity behaviours such as shadow-copy deletion and PsExec lateral movement carries near-zero discriminating value.

Weighting: Halcyon's independently published assessment directly contradicts this claim. Where a multi-vendor-corroborated "no connection" assessment conflicts with a single unconfirmed TTP-similarity claim from a commercial MSP blog, the independent-origin assessment carries substantially more weight. The BlackCat claim is recorded here for completeness and is not treated as a lineage finding.
Vendor Designation Disambiguation
Coverage gap, not a designation gap: No CrowdStrike, Microsoft, Mandiant, Secureworks, or Unit 42 group-specific tracking name for DireWolf was identified in open reporting. The group is absent from the Unit 42 tracked threat actor index. This means there is no SPIDER, TEMPEST, UNC, or GOLD designation to disambiguate, and no parent-organisation designation that could be conflated with a group-specific one. The absence is itself a finding: after fifteen months and 100 claimed victims, DireWolf sits below the visibility threshold that triggers a distinct actor codename at the five largest attribution vendors, which limits cross-vendor triangulation on every other question in this profile.
03

Operational Model

Structure: Closed Crew
Credible

Halcyon assesses DireWolf as a "Closed Group operating as tight core team without affiliate recruitment or RaaS infrastructure," estimating three to seven operators comprising one to two developers, two to three network operators, and one to two negotiators. SOCRadar reaches the same conclusion independently, citing per-victim customised builds, personalised ransom notes with unique logins, and a private Tor negotiation panel. Proven Data describes "controlled, operator-driven execution rather than automated spread." [4][5][9]

The structural markers cited across these three sources are consistent: unified technical characteristics across all samples, centralised rather than distributed infrastructure, and no observed recruitment advertising on criminal forums.

The Competing "Semi-Closed RaaS" Characterisation
Analyst Inference, single source, no evidence cited

Security Arsenal characterises DireWolf in August 2026 as a "closed/semi-private group with affiliate recruitment observed on Russian-language forums; functions effectively as RaaS with a curated affiliate base rather than open enrollment." No forum, thread, handle, date, or screenshot is cited in support. The claim appears in an attribute table within an article whose stated source is "ransomware.live dark web leak site monitoring," that is, leak-site scraping rather than forum collection.

Handling: This claim is retained because a genuine closed-to-RaaS transition would be operationally significant and would explain the August 2026 volume spike. It is not treated as established. The evidentiary basis is a single commercial MSP blog with no cited collection, published fourteen months after the last independent structural assessment. If corroborated, it would also be the first Russian-language-ecosystem evidence for this group, which is why it matters disproportionately to Section 09.
Negotiation and Extortion Mechanics
Confirmed
  • Pre-encryption reconnaissance: days to weeks between initial breach and detonation, evidenced by per-victim encryptor compilation with the target organisation's name hardcoded into the ransom note.
  • Exfiltration: approximately 265 GB average per intrusion, with proof samples uploaded to gofile.io before the encryptor runs.
  • Contact: Tox messenger using a published qTox ID, plus a victim-specific Tor negotiation room with a hardcoded room ID, username and password embedded in the note.
  • Timeline: a short confidentiality window of a few days, then roughly thirty days before the complete data set is published.
  • Publication: sample data and a file inventory posted first as proof, full dump on deadline expiry.

No triple-extortion behaviour (DDoS, direct customer or regulator notification) has been documented for DireWolf by any source reviewed. [1][2][4][5]

04

Technical Capabilities

Initial Access: The Central Intelligence Gap
Confirmed (negative finding)

Halcyon states the position plainly: "Unknown initial access vectors represent critical intelligence gap." Trustwave's June 2025 analysis concluded that "no initial access, reconnaissance or lateral movement techniques used by Dire Wolf are known at this point." SOCRadar lists spear-phishing, exposed RDP or VPN, weak credentials, and possible third-party or managed service provider compromise as likely, but explicitly notes "no confirmed cases have been published." [1][4][5]

Halcyon's exploits table records: No CVEs Documented. Searches of CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog and NIST National Vulnerability Database contain no associations. Halcyon offers three candidate explanations for that absence: zero-day use, purely credential-based access, or operational security sufficient to prevent intelligence collection. [4]

The CVE-2026-50751 Attribution
Analyst Inference, single source, misattributed elsewhere

Security Arsenal names CVE-2026-50751 (Check Point Security Gateway improper authentication in IKEv1) as DireWolf's "priority one" edge vector, alongside CVE-2026-20131 (Cisco Secure FMC), CVE-2023-21529 (Microsoft Exchange), CVE-2024-1708 (ConnectWise ScreenConnect) and CVE-2026-48027 (Nx Console). Three findings materially change how that list should be read.

CheckResult

CWE-287 improper authentication, CVSS 9.3, affecting Remote Access VPN, Mobile Access and Spark Firewall where deprecated IKEv1 is enabled. Added to the CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog with an 11 June 2026 remediation deadline. A companion flaw, CVE-2026-50752 (CVSS 7.4), affects the same code path.
Is it linked to ransomware?Yes, but not to DireWolf. Rapid7's exploitation reporting attributes at least one observed incident to a Qilin ransomware affiliate. No vendor has published DireWolf exploitation of this CVE.
What is the basis for the DireWolf linkage?KEV correlation, not telemetry. Security Arsenal presents the list under the heading "Probable Initial Access Vectors (CISA KEV correlation)" and states the entries "map cleanly to DIREWOLF's known access profile." This is the vendor reasoning from the KEV catalogue toward a group whose access profile is, by every other vendor's account, unknown.
Analyst judgement: The CVE is real, critical, KEV-listed and worth patching on its own merits. The DireWolf attribution is not evidence. Treating this list as DireWolf's access profile would create a false sense that the central intelligence gap has closed, when it has not. Collection requirement: incident-response telemetry from a confirmed DireWolf intrusion establishing the entry vector.

  • Partial encryption: files under 1 MB are fully encrypted; files above 1 MB have only the first 1 MB encrypted. AhnLab assesses this delivers protection comparable to full encryption while sharply increasing throughput across large enterprise data sets.
  • Command-line control: no configuration file. -d targets a specific path, -h prints help. With no argument, all local and network drives except CD-ROM are traversed.
  • Exclusions: Windows, Program Files, AppData, $Recycle.Bin, System Volume Information; bootmgr, ntldr, NTUSER.DAT; extensions .exe .dll .sys .drv .bin .tmp .iso .img and .direwolf. These preserve a bootable system so the ransom note can be read and payment made.
  • Single-run guard: mutex Global\direwolfAppMutex plus marker file C:\runfinish.exe. If either exists the binary self-deletes and exits.

AhnLab's conclusion is that this design "effectively blocks all known decryption methods, leaving victims with no means of recovery other than negotiating with the threat actor." [1][2][4]

Anti-Forensics and Anti-Recovery
Confirmed

The event-log suppression routine is the most operationally distinctive behaviour in the sample and the highest-value detection opportunity. Rather than clearing logs once, the malware runs an infinite loop: a WMI query retrieves the eventlog service PID, taskkill /f /pid terminates it, the loop waits, then repeats. Even if the service restarts it is killed again, so logging stays down for the duration of the intrusion. [1][2]

Platform Coverage
Confirmed: Windows only

Golang gives theoretical cross-platform portability, but Trustwave, AhnLab and Halcyon all report Windows-only variants in the wild. Halcyon states explicitly: "only Windows variants observed with no Linux or ESXi versions." Security Arsenal's August 2026 assertion of "evidence of ESXi/Linux variants in some intrusions" is contradicted by all three technical analyses and cites no sample, hash, or incident. It is not accepted here. [1][2][4]

Why this matters: The absence of an ESXi encryptor is a meaningful capability ceiling. Groups that scale into large enterprise environments almost invariably build a hypervisor variant, because encrypting a handful of ESXi hosts is faster and more damaging than encrypting thousands of Windows guests. An ESXi build appearing in the wild would be the clearest single indicator that DireWolf is investing in scale, and is a priority collection tripwire.
CIS Targeting Behaviour
Confirmed (negative finding)

No source reviewed, including the binary-level analyses by Trustwave and AhnLab and the technical breakdowns by Halcyon and CSA, documents a Russian keyboard-layout check, a CIS locale exclusion, or any geographic kill switch in the DireWolf encryptor. The exclusion logic that is present is functional (system paths and file types needed to keep the host bootable), not geographic.

This is analytically significant. The keyboard or locale kill switch is the single most consistent fingerprint of Russian-speaking ransomware operations, and comparable checks are documented for peer groups: TRM records Sarcoma deliberately avoiding Uzbekistan, and Conti-lineage families including Akira carry explicit CIS exclusions. Its absence in an otherwise mature Golang implementation weighs against, without disproving, placement in the traditional Russian-speaking cybercrime ecosystem. The finding is a documented absence in published analysis rather than a confirmed absence in the binary, and would be strengthened by a dedicated reverse-engineering statement. [1][2][4][6]

Defensive Mapping

DireWolf's detection profile is unusual: the initial-access half of the kill chain is undocumented, while the impact half is exhaustively documented down to individual command lines. Defenders therefore cannot build a reliable early-warning tripwire from published DireWolf intelligence, but they can build a very high-fidelity late-stage one. The practical consequence is that detection engineering for this group should focus on the pre-detonation window, and prevention should focus on recovery capability that survives the encryptor.

Top Defensive Priorities
  • Immutable or offline backups. DireWolf's playbook is shadow-copy-first and backup-service-first. Every recovery mechanism it destroys is one that lives on the production network. Object-lock or air-gapped copies defeat the entire anti-recovery routine.
  • Alert on eventlog service termination. The repeated WMI-plus-taskkill loop against the eventlog service is close to a signature. Legitimate administration does not kill the event log service in a loop.
  • Off-host log forwarding. Because the malware suppresses local logging before it does anything destructive, a sudden stop in log flow from a host is itself the alert.
  • Block anonymous file hosts. gofile.io is the confirmed proof-of-exfiltration channel and is a low-cost egress block.
  • MFA on all remote access. With the entry vector unknown, credential-based access must be assumed and closed by default.
  • Capture the sample before reboot. The forced reboot plus self-deletion is specifically designed to deny responders the binary. EDR quarantine-on-execute preserves it.
Leverage note: The financial layer is the softest surface on this group. TRM Labs and Halcyon independently describe a laundering path with limited obfuscation and a "potentially recoverable cash-out strategy," running through no-KYC non-custodial exchanges. Unlike the technical layer, which is well engineered, and the attribution layer, which is empty, the payment path is documented, traceable and unactioned. Exchange-level pressure is the available lever.

Financial Infrastructure

Payment Model
Credible

Bitcoin is the primary demanded asset. Halcyon records "Bitcoin primary cryptocurrency with deposits to non-custodial exchanges requiring no KYC." Security Arsenal additionally references Monero acceptance in 2026; this is single-source and unverified. No documentation exists of the wallet-address structure (single address per victim versus a rotating pool), which limits any assessment of clustering opportunities. [4][10]

On-Chain Laundering
Credible (TRM Labs, primary)

TRM Labs profiled DireWolf in its October 2025 report "Nine Emerging Groups Shaping the Ransomware Landscape." The assessment is short and specific: "Dire Wolf typically launders victim proceeds downstream, making multiple deposits into a non-custodial exchange," reflecting "a desire to quickly convert or swap assets without undergoing strict KYC procedures, allowing the group to maintain anonymity while moving funds across chains or into privacy-enhancing assets." [6]

1
Victim payment received in Bitcoin
Paid to operator-controlled addresses following Tox or Tor-portal negotiation. No intermediary payment processor documented.
2
Downstream multiple deposits into a non-custodial exchange
TRM's distinguishing observation. DireWolf splits proceeds into multiple deposits rather than the single-funnel pattern TRM documents for Arkana Security, or the peel-chain-into-mixer pattern it documents for AiLock and APTLock.
3
Cross-chain swap or conversion to privacy assets
TRM assesses intent to swap across chains or into privacy-enhancing assets. The specific destination assets and chains are not published for DireWolf.
Comparative placement (TRM's own framing): DireWolf's laundering is assessed as unsophisticated relative to its technical capability. Halcyon's summary of the blockchain picture is that it shows "limited obfuscation with potentially recoverable cash-out strategy," and that the "straightforward approach making multiple deposits reflects quick conversion priority but creates increased traceability." A group that engineered a clean Curve25519 implementation is cashing out through no-KYC venues with minimal layering.
Absence of Cross-Group Financial Linkage
Confirmed

This is the strongest single piece of evidence for independent origin and deserves separate treatment. In the same TRM report, Frag is assessed as possibly "an extension of the Akira ransomware group," sharing a two-address wallet cluster plus the same bridge and payment service; Kairos is documented sharing a global VASP cash-out address with SafePay, INC, Lynx and Qilin. DireWolf appears in that same comparative analysis with no such linkage recorded. Halcyon states it directly: "minimal on-chain connections through common non-custodial exchanges without shared wallets or financial links to established groups." [4][6]

The distinction matters: sharing a common non-custodial exchange is a coincidence of venue, whereas sharing a wallet cluster or a payment service is a relationship. DireWolf has the former and not the latter.

Singapore CSA Alert AL-2025-082
Confirmed

The Cyber Security Agency of Singapore published "Ongoing Dire Wolf Ransomware Campaign" on 18 August 2025. It remains the only government product naming this group anywhere in the world. Halcyon confirms it as the "only government-level threat assessment to date." [3][4]

  • Describes double extortion, anti-forensics and the multi-stage attack chain
  • Publishes both packed and unpacked data345.exe hashes as IOCs
  • Directs administrators to monitor for those IOCs and to maintain tested backups
  • Cites Broadcom, Trustwave SpiderLabs and an AlienVault OTX pulse as its references
  • Contains no enforcement, attribution, or victim-solicitation component

Singapore's issuance is explained by victimology rather than jurisdiction: SOCRadar assessed Singapore as the single largest victim country as of September 2025, and Halcyon put approximately 60% of victims in Asia-Pacific. The alert is a defensive response to a national exposure concentration, not the product of an investigation. [4][5]

Assessment: Why No Action
Analyst Inference

Fifteen months, 100 claimed victims across 32 countries, zero enforcement actions. Compared against peer groups disrupted within similar windows (LockBit via Operation Cronos, 8Base via Operation Phobos Aetor, Royal and BlackSuit via Operation Checkmate), the absence is conspicuous and has several candidate explanations, none of which can be confirmed from open sources.

HypothesisSupporting evidenceCounter-evidence
Below the threshold of attentionNo large-vendor tracking designation; no CISA advisory; no Western critical-infrastructure incident of political salience; victim concentration initially in Asia-Pacific rather than the US or EU100 victims and a US-first 2026 distribution should now clear most thresholds

Structural finding: DireWolf is a test case for whether an operation can reach three figures of victims while remaining beneath the attribution and enforcement threshold purely by staying small, staying off forums, avoiding a rebrand, and avoiding politically salient targets. Fifteen months of open-source silence suggests the answer is currently yes. The measurable indicator to watch is whether the August 2026 US-weighted surge and the first education-sector victim (Arizona State University) change that.

Attribution and State Nexus

Jurisdictional Assessment: Unresolved
Credible at best, single vendor

DireWolf's jurisdiction is genuinely open. The evidentiary position is thinner than for almost any comparably active group.

Signal 01
The New York claim is false
The leak site states "Based in New York, NY." Trustwave describes the claimed location as "not credible"; SOCRadar calls it "unverified and likely misdirection"; Halcyon calls it misdirection. Three independent vendors reject it. That establishes deliberate deception about location but says nothing about the true one. CONFIRMED as a false claim.
Signal 02
Linguistic assessment
Halcyon states that "linguistic patterns suggest possible Eastern European or Russian-speaking origins." SOCRadar notes the site and notes are written in English that is "generally fluent with occasional non-native phrasing," without naming a language family. This is the only positive jurisdiction signal in open reporting and it is one vendor's judgement, unsupported by published examples. CREDIBLE, single source.
Signal 03
Russian-language forum recruitment
Security Arsenal's August 2026 assertion of affiliate recruitment on Russian-language forums, if corroborated, would place the group in the Russian-speaking ecosystem. No forum, thread, handle or date is cited, and the article's stated collection source is leak-site monitoring. ANALYST INFERENCE, uncorroborated.
Counter-signal
No CIS exclusion in the binary
No keyboard-layout check, locale check, or CIS geographic exclusion is documented in any published analysis of the encryptor, including two binary-level teardowns. This is the standard fingerprint of Russian-speaking operations and it is absent. It weighs against, without disproving, the Russian-ecosystem placement suggested by Signals 02 and 03. CONFIRMED absence in published analysis.
Russian Intelligence Services Nexus
Confirmed (negative finding)

No confirmed or vendor-assessed connection to the FSB, SVR, GRU, or any other state intelligence service exists for DireWolf. No government advisory alleges state sponsorship. No vendor has proposed a state-nexus hypothesis. TRM Labs, which in the same October 2025 report assessed APTLock as reportedly linked to a Russian state-sponsored group, made no such suggestion regarding DireWolf and placed it in the purely financially motivated tier. [6]

The distinction from Conti-lineage groups is stark. For Akira, three independent evidentiary pillars support Russian-speaking, Russia-based attribution: blockchain flows to Conti-linked wallets, Russian-language forum communication, and a hardcoded Russian keyboard kill switch. DireWolf has none of the three. Its on-chain profile is unlinked, its forum presence is unevidenced, and its binary carries no locale check.

Named Individuals
Confirmed (negative finding)

No indictment, government advisory, or credible vendor report has publicly named any individual as a DireWolf operator, developer, negotiator, or affiliate. There is no handle, no alias, no forum persona, and no OPSEC failure in the public record. This is the deepest attribution gap in the profile, and it is what makes the group's persistence structurally interesting rather than merely unremarkable.

Explicit Conclusion
Assessed analytic line: DireWolf is a financially motivated criminal operation of unresolved jurisdiction. Its own claimed location (New York) is confirmed false. A Russian-speaking or Eastern European origin is the leading hypothesis but rests on one vendor's linguistic judgement plus one uncorroborated forum claim, and is partially undercut by the documented absence of the CIS exclusion logic that almost every Russian-speaking family carries. No state nexus of any kind is confirmed, alleged, or hypothesised by any vendor. Jurisdiction should be treated as an open collection requirement rather than a settled attribute, and this profile deliberately declines to place the group in the Russia/CIS ecosystem on the evidence currently available.

Trajectory Assessment

Operational Tempo: Burst and Dormancy, Not Steady Growth
Confirmed

This is the profile's most significant correction to prevailing open reporting. Cumulative victim totals rising from 6 to 100 look like steady expansion. The post-date record shows something quite different.

WindowPostsCharacter
May to Jun 20256, then 16Launch burst
Jul to Nov 2025to 41+Sustained, roughly 7 per month (Halcyon)
4 to 13 Jan 202612Cluster
14 Jan to 11 Jun 20260Five-month dormancy
12 Jun 20264Single-day burst
13 Jun to 9 Aug 20260Two-month dormancy
10 to 19 Aug 202625Surge: five batch drops in ten days

Total 2026 output is 41 posts, of which 25 fall inside a ten-day window. Proven Data's 10 August 2026 snapshot independently confirms the pattern by recording "0 in the last 30 days" on that date, immediately before the surge began. [7][8][9]

Analytic implication: Descriptions of DireWolf as maintaining "sustained, undiminished operational tempo" through 2026 are not supported by the posting record. The group went dark for seven of the first seven and a half months of 2026. Batch drops of this shape usually indicate one of three things: a negotiation deadline wall hitting a cohort simultaneously, a deliberate credibility-reassertion event after a quiet period, or a payout dispute triggering mass publication. All three imply intrusions accumulated silently during the dormant windows, which means dwell time and unposted victim counts are both being systematically understated by leak-site-derived metrics.
Infrastructure Stability
Credible

RansomLook records approximately 53% average uptime for the DireWolf onion over the trailing 30 days and flags a degraded parser state; ransomware.live recorded the site as unreachable at 20 Aug 2026 16:03 UTC. Intermittent availability of this degree is not characteristic of a well-resourced operation running redundant mirrors, and is consistent with the small-crew assessment. It is not evidence of law enforcement pressure, of which there is none. [7][8]

Capability Evolution
Confirmed: none observed

Halcyon's finding is direct: "The group maintains consistent technical approaches with no tactical evolution since emergence. Unlike many ransomware families releasing multiple versions, refined processes from day one with zero reported technical failures suggest immediate maturity." Fifteen months on, there is still no second encryptor version, no ESXi or Linux build, no observed change in the encryption scheme, and no change in the extortion timeline. [4]

The operational-model question is the only live evolution hypothesis: a shift from closed crew toward curated affiliates would explain the August 2026 volume without any change in tooling. It remains single-sourced and unverified (Section 03).

Trajectory Indicators
  • Volume, rising but discontinuous. 100 posts cumulative, with 2026 output concentrated into three short bursts separated by two multi-month silences.
  • Geography, globalising. The 60% Asia-Pacific concentration of late 2025 has broadened; the US is now first and Malaysia has entered at second.
  • Sector, shifting toward regulated data. Healthcare has moved into the top three, with a hospital operator, a medical device maker and a healthcare staffing firm all posted in a single August 2026 batch. No sector moratorium exists.
  • Tooling, static. No version iteration, no hypervisor variant, no new capability in fifteen months. An ESXi build would be the clearest scale-investment tripwire.
  • Brand, stable. No rebrand, no alias, no exit-scam signal, no internal leak, no affiliate dispute in public reporting. A single consistent identity since May 2025.
  • Disruption, absent. No degradation attributable to law enforcement. Infrastructure instability is self-inflicted or resourcing-related, not imposed.
Connected Group Cluster
Confirmed: no cluster membership

Applying two-tier confidence: there is no anchor relationship to assess, and therefore no extension claims either. No shared infrastructure, shared affiliate, shared laundering path, or code reuse links DireWolf to Conti, REvil, LockBit, Black Basta, Akira, Hive, ALPHV, or any other named family. DireWolf does not appear on published lineage or cluster diagrams. The single BlackCat/ALPHV TTP-overlap suggestion is addressed and discounted in Section 02.

Vendor publication status: Neither Mandiant nor Recorded Future has published a formal assessment of DireWolf or of any cluster relationship involving it. Neither has CrowdStrike, Microsoft, Secureworks, or Unit 42. The independent-origin finding rests on Halcyon and TRM Labs, corroborated by the absence of contrary evidence in Trustwave, AhnLab, SOCRadar and CSA reporting. That is a reasonable evidentiary base but it is not the multi-vendor consensus that a comparable finding for a Conti-lineage group would carry.
Key Intelligence Gaps and Collection Requirements
Priority collection requirements, in order of analytic value:
  • Initial access vector. Incident-response telemetry from a confirmed DireWolf intrusion establishing entry. This is the single largest gap and every defensive recommendation upstream of the encryptor is currently guesswork.
  • Named receiving exchange. TRM describes the venue type but not the venue. Without the name, no exchange-level pressure recommendation can be made specific, which leaves the group's softest surface untouched.
  • Jurisdiction. One vendor's linguistic judgement is the entire positive evidence base. Forum collection, infrastructure registration data, or operator-side OPSEC failure would resolve it.
  • Independent on-chain corroboration. Chainalysis or Elliptic publication would move the laundering assessment from single-origin to corroborated.
  • Binary-level statement on CIS exclusion. The absence is currently inferred from silence across four analyses rather than asserted by a reverse engineer with sample access.
  • Payment and settlement data. One observed ransom figure from June 2025 is the entire negotiation evidence base. No payment rate, no settlement ratio, no revenue estimate exists.
  • Dwell-time measurement. The dormancy-then-batch posting pattern means leak-site dates are a poor proxy for intrusion dates, and the true compromise-to-publication interval is unmeasured.
