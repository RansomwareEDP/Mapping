01

Executive Summary and Group Overview

Rhysida is a double-extortion ransomware brand first seen in May 2023 and still operating on 29 October 2026, with 307 lifetime leak-site listings and new posts on 27, 28 and 29 October 2026. The brand is operated by a long-running crew that previously ran Vice Society and before that deployed Zeppelin and HelloKitty lockers. That crew is tracked as VICE SPIDER (CrowdStrike), GOLD VICTOR (Secureworks, now Sophos), Vanilla Tempest, formerly DEV-0832 (Microsoft), and Arcane Mantis (PRODAFT). The lineage rests on intrusion-cluster and tooling continuity documented by Sophos, Check Point and Secureworks; no vendor has published code, blockchain or personnel evidence linking the two brands. [9][10][12][13][16] Confirmed: same operators Inference: single crew, not a rebrand

Three things distinguish Rhysida from the RaaS mainstream. First, its leak site runs seven-day auctions that sell each stolen dataset to a single "exclusive" buyer, with lots marked sold or published if unsold. Second, its operating model is contested: CISA and most press call it RaaS, but Secureworks has never seen affiliate advertising and CrowdStrike states that VICE SPIDER "privately operates both Rhysida and Interlock ransomware and the associated dedicated leak sites." Third, since 2024 its intake has shifted from stolen VPN credentials to malvertising and SEO-poisoned fake installers (Teams, Zoom, PuTTY) delivering the OysterLoader backdoor, signed with fraudulently obtained code-signing certificates. [9][10][20][33] Confirmed

Government output is thin. One joint advisory exists (CISA, FBI and MS-ISAC AA23-319A, November 2023, updated April 2025) plus an HHS sector alert. There are no arrests, indictments, sanctions, rewards or seizures naming Rhysida, Vice Society or any of the vendor-tracked operator names. The only court action is civil: Microsoft's May 2026 case in the Southern District of New York against the Fox Tempest code-signing service names "Vanilla Tempest" as John Does 3 and 4, the first court document to name the Rhysida operators. In the same period Microsoft revoked over 200 certificates used in Rhysida's Teams-installer campaign (October 2025) and over 1,000 Fox Tempest certificates (May 2026). The group kept listing victims through both actions, including the Berlin state administration (30 BTC demand, 5.79 TB published) and Anne Arundel County, Maryland (25 BTC, outcome unresolved). [1][4][5][6][21][37][39] Confirmed

Tempo read, 29 October 2026: the internal victim database shows 35 Rhysida listings in the 90 days to 29 October against 4 in the preceding 90, with 12 in September and 16 in October to date. Aggregator commentary from 2025 describing Rhysida as "significantly less active" is out of date. The crew is in a posting surge, concentrated on US local government, legal services and German public bodies. Internal [45][46]

02

Lineage and Organizational Heritage

Core Assessment
Confirmed: Vice Society operators moved to the Rhysida payload
Credible: a single crew spanning Zeppelin, HelloKitty, Vice Society, Rhysida and INC

Vice Society ran from mid 2021 to June 2023 without an encryptor of its own, deploying Zeppelin and HelloKitty/FiveHands and concentrating on education and healthcare. Its leak site stopped posting on 21 June 2023, weeks after Rhysida's first listings. Sophos tracked six intrusions between November 2022 and June 2023 by one cluster (TAC5279) that switched from Vice Society to Rhysida payloads and asserted "with high confidence that the TAC5279 affiliate group has transitioned to deploying the Rhysida ransomware variant", while stating it had no evidence that Vice Society had "rebranded". Check Point reached "at least medium confidence that Vice Society operators are now using Rhysida ransomware" and noted the same NTDS dump paths, SystemBC persistence and PortStarter use. Secureworks assesses with moderate confidence that GOLD VICTOR "shifted to an operation built around Rhysida ransomware" in mid 2023. CrowdStrike and Microsoft fold the whole history into one actor (VICE SPIDER; Vanilla Tempest). [9][10][12][13][23]

The distinction that matters: every vendor describes operators changing payloads, not a RaaS program renaming itself. Vice Society never had its own locker to rebrand. Check Point adds that others could also be using Rhysida, which is why "same crew" is CONFIRMED for the transition and only CREDIBLE for exclusivity. [12][13]

Hand-Off in the Leak-Site Record
Vice Society Hands Off to Rhysida, October 2022 to June 2023 Check Point's monthly leak-site counts: Vice Society posts through May 2023 (3 that month), Rhysida appears with about 20 in May and 17 in June 2023, and Vice Society's last post follows on 21 June 2023. Evidentiary Pillars
Pillar 1: Confirmed
Intrusion-Cluster Continuity
Six Sophos cases, November 2022 to June 2023, one cluster: VPN access without MFA, ZeroLogon (CVE-2020-1472), PortStarter leading to SystemBC, then Vice Society payloads early and Rhysida payloads late. Sophos: high confidence on the transition. [12]
Pillar 2: Confirmed
Tooling and Artifact Continuity
Shared NTDS.dit dump path (C:\Users\Public\temp_l0gs), SystemBC "socks" Run key persistence, PortStarter, a firewall rule named "Windows Update", a domain-wide password reset before encryption, and the same onionmail.org contact pattern. Check Point, Secureworks and Recorded Future report overlapping subsets. [10][13][18]
Pillar 3: Confirmed
Timing and Victimology
Rhysida's first listings (May to June 2023) immediately precede Vice Society's last post (21 June 2023). Sector focus (education, healthcare) carried over unchanged. Circumstantial on its own, decisive in combination with pillars 1 and 2. [3][13]
Pillars absent: Gap
No Code, Blockchain or Personnel Evidence
Code overlap would not be expected (Vice Society used third-party lockers; Rhysida is a new MinGW/LibTomCrypt build). No public wallet clustering connects the two brands' payments. No named individuals, handles or chat leaks exist for either. The lineage is behavioural, not forensic. [1][24]

03

Operational Model

RaaS or Closed Crew: An Unresolved Conflict
Credible: private or closed operation

CISA's advisory states that Rhysida "operates in a ransomware-as-a-service (RaaS) model" with profit-sharing between developers and affiliates, citing open-source reporting; Kaspersky and Recorded Future repeat the label, the latter with "allegedly". Against that, Secureworks has never observed affiliate advertising for Vice Society or Rhysida and writes that Rhysida "does not appear to be operated as ransomware-as-a-service"; CrowdStrike states the operator "privately operates" the brand and its leak site; IBM reaches the same conclusion for the sibling brand Interlock. No forum recruitment post, affiliate panel, vetting rule or revenue split has ever been published for Rhysida. The RaaS label traces to early 2023 press reporting and has no primary evidence behind it. This profile treats Rhysida as a closed or private operation that buys access and tooling from suppliers rather than recruiting affiliates. [1][9][10][17][24]

Supplier Model: Access and Tooling Bought In

What the open record does show is a supplier chain rather than an affiliate roster. Initial access has come from Gootloader operators (Microsoft's Storm-0494 hand-off, September 2024; Gootloader added to the CISA advisory in April 2025), from the TAG-124 traffic distribution system (Recorded Future), and from the UNC6016 malvertising cluster (Mandiant, March 2026). Code-signing came from the Fox Tempest service, of which Vanilla Tempest was a customer from June 2025 until Microsoft's seizure in May 2026. The loader (OysterLoader, also called CleanUpLoader and Broomstick) is closely linked to the ITG23 Trickbot/Conti developer lineage per IBM and Recorded Future, and is used by other groups, so the supplier relationship is shared rather than exclusive. [1][6][15][17][18][19][22]

Extortion and Negotiation
  • Contact. The ransom note (CriticalBreachDetected.pdf) carries a unique key and a Tor portal address and states no price. Contact addresses follow an onionmail.org pattern. Payment is in Bitcoin only. [1][2]
  • Auction mechanics. Rapid7 (November 2025): auctions "since at least June 2023", typically seven days, each dataset "sold exclusively to a single buyer"; as of mid October 2025 five auctions ran concurrently with starting prices of 5 to 10 BTC. Unsold data is published; the Lurie Children's listing threatened sale to multiple buyers at a lower price if the auction failed. [33]
  • Media pressure. Sophos (December 2023): the leak-site contact form lists journalists first, before "Recoveries", and the site solicits media and "fans". Press attention is part of the pressure model. [34]
  • Negotiation. Fortinet reports the crew negotiates unless a counteroffer is "insultingly low"; no reduction ranges are published. The Port of Seattle leak included what the group said was a 750,000 US dollar counteroffer from the Port, which is unverified. [28][44]
  • Data selection. HR shares are targeted for passports, driving licences and identity documents (Recorded Future). At the British Library about 60 percent of copied data came from Finance, Technology and People team drives, the rest from keyword searches such as "passport" and "confidential". [8][18]
  • No third tier. No DDoS or direct harassment of victims' customers is documented. [1][33]

Post-Exploitation Tradecraft
  • Persistence and C2: SystemBC with a "socks" Run key; PortStarter; AnyDesk; Supper (also SocksShell, WINDYTWIST) backdoor, first seen July 2024 and shared with Interlock intrusions; NetSupport assessed likely; signed OysterLoader binaries. [1][13][17][18][20]
  • Scheduled tasks: a task named "AlphaSecurity" (IBM); in one 2026 incident the LIONSHARE tunneler ran every 12 hours as SYSTEM via rundll32 loading red.dll (Mandiant). [15][17]
  • Credential access and discovery: secretsdump; ntdsutil backups of NTDS.dit to the temp_l0gs folder; PowerView. [1][13]
  • Lateral movement: RDP, PsExec, PuTTY and SSH, PowerShell and WMI; Cobalt Strike. [1]
  • Exfiltration: AzCopy and Azure Storage Explorer, MEGA, WinSCP; staging in C:\in and C:\out; one At-Bay case moved about 100,000 files with AzCopy. [1][30]
  • Defence evasion and impact: wevtutil log clearing; shadow-copy deletion; a domain-wide password reset immediately before encryption; Wake-on-LAN to reach powered-off hosts; the SILENTKILL script; at the British Library, servers were destroyed to block recovery and forensics. [8][29][30]
Encryptor
  • Build: 64-bit Windows PE compiled with MinGW/GCC, using LibTomCrypt. [1][24]
  • Scheme: code-level analyses (Kookmin University and KISA, Gen Digital, Recorded Future) show AES-256-CTR file encryption with per-file keys from a ChaCha20-based PRNG, wrapped by an embedded per-sample RSA-4096 OAEP public key and stored in a 0x40C-byte footer. CISA and HHS describe the scheme as "4096-bit RSA encryption key with the ChaCha20 algorithm"; the code analyses are the more precise description. [1][2][25][26]
  • Speed: intermittent and multithreaded. Files under 1 MiB are fully encrypted; larger files in chunks (four 1 MiB blocks for files of 4 MiB or more). [26]
  • Output: .rhysida extension; system-folder and extension exclusion lists; wallpaper change; PowerShell self-deletion; drive order changed in builds after 1 July 2023. [24][25]
  • Linux/ESXi: ELF variant observed around November 2023, targeting /vmfs/volumes and standard Linux paths while skipping ESXi boot.cfg; arguments include -d (directory), -sr (self-remove), nobg and -md5. Fortinet saw ESXi hosts encrypted first in one incident. [28][29]
Decryptor Availability
Confirmed: public decryptor for early Windows builds

In early builds the PRNG was seeded with a predictable time-based value, so file modification times allow the seed, key and IV to be regenerated. Kookmin University and KISA published the method (arXiv, February 2024) and KISA released a free Windows decryptor hosted on its own site; Emsisoft's Fabian Wosar noted it works only on files encrypted by the Windows encryptor. Recorded Future reported in October 2024 that builds from June 2024 seed the PRNG from runtime process data and are not vulnerable; that statement comes from a report this profile could not re-read in full and is carried as CREDIBLE. No newer public decryptor exists. [18][26][27]

CIS Exclusion

No source documents a keyboard-layout or language kill switch in the encryptor. The code-level analyses reviewed (CISA, Gen Digital, Kookmin/KISA, Kaspersky, Fortinet, Trend Micro, Recorded Future) say nothing about GetKeyboardLayoutList or locale checks. Claims of CIS avoidance are about targeting, not binary behaviour: HHS says victim geography "loosely aligns" with CIS avoidance; ransomware.live records no CIS victim among 42 countries; Trend Micro's detection telemetry led with Jordan, the United States and Indonesia. This is absence of evidence. A current sample's import table and strings should be checked directly before the point is asserted either way. [2][24][29][45] Analyst inference: targeting-level avoidance only

Credible: Russian-speaking eCrime

Structural Leverage Points

This section records where the operation's structure is exposed, on open-source evidence only, and what observable change would show that a given dependency has been degraded. Every point is tied to a cited fact and a measurable indicator. It describes exposure; it does not assign tasks. Priority order reflects impact against reversibility.

Point 1: highest value
A single operator-controlled payment and auction channel
Exposure: one Tor portal sets every price and receives every payment; one leak site runs every auction. No affiliate layer disperses the money. Whatever the RaaS label says, the structure is a closed crew with one cash intake.
Evidence: CISA on the portal model [1]; Secureworks and CrowdStrike on private operation [9][10]; Rapid7 on the auction mechanics [33].
Indicator: inbound consolidation from many victims to a small address set once any Rhysida payment address becomes public; auction completion rate ("sold" markers) over time.
Confidence: CONFIRMED on the model; no wallet is public.
Point 2
Dependence on bought-in access and signing
Exposure: since 2024 the crew's intake has run through third parties: Gootloader operators, the TAG-124 TDS, the UNC6016 malvertising cluster and the Fox Tempest signing service. The May 2026 seizure proved the suppliers are reachable; the three-month trough that followed suggests the dependency is real, and the subsequent surge suggests substitutes exist.
Evidence: Microsoft DCU and court orders [4][5][6]; Expel certificate tracking [20]; Mandiant UNC6016 [15]; Recorded Future TAG-124 [19]; CISA Gootloader update [1].
Indicator: listing rate in the 90 days after each supplier action; share of new Rhysida intrusions entering via signed installers versus credentials in vendor case reporting; certificate issuance cadence to the crew's loaders.
Confidence: CONFIRMED.
Point 3
The buyer side of the auction model
Exposure: the exclusive-buyer auction only pays if buyers exist. Every public case shows refusal by the victim and publication by the crew; no buyer has ever been identified. If the buyer market is thin or fictional, the auction is a pressure tactic rather than a revenue line, and the crew's income depends entirely on victim payments it cannot publicly evidence.
Evidence: Rapid7 auction documentation [33]; the demand table in Section 03; absence of any confirmed sale or payment in open source.
Indicator: ratio of "sold" markers to published datasets; time between auction expiry and publication; any buyer-side identification by researchers.
Confidence: CREDIBLE on the mechanics; the thin-market reading is ANALYST INFERENCE.
Point 4
A crew named in court and nowhere else
Exposure: Vanilla Tempest is a named defendant in a US federal civil case with a signed preliminary injunction, FBI and Europol participation, and declarations tying specific domains to the defendants. No criminal, sanctions or reward action builds on it. The discovery and service record is the most advanced public attribution of the operators and currently carries no consequence beyond certificate revocation.
Evidence: Microsoft v. John Doe 1 et al., 26 Civ. 3737 [5]; Microsoft Security Blog on Fox Tempest's cryptocurrency links [6].
Indicator: any designation, indictment or reward that cites the case; unsealing of further declarations; changes in Teams-installer infrastructure naming after the injunction.
Confidence: CONFIRMED.
Point 5
Two brands, one developer
Exposure: if CrowdStrike and PRODAFT are right that one operator or developer runs both Rhysida and Interlock, then shared components (Supper, exclusion lists, the TDS) are single points of failure for both brands, and action against either leak site tests the claim directly.
Evidence: CrowdStrike VICE SPIDER [9]; PRODAFT March 2025 statement [16]; IBM and Forescout on shared tooling [17][32]; Secureworks and IBM dissent on operator identity [11][17].
Indicator: correlated tempo changes across the two leak sites; simultaneous tooling changes (new backdoor, new loader) on both; any infrastructure overlap surfaced by a takedown of one.
Confidence: ANALYST INFERENCE built on a VENDOR CONFLICT.
Point 6
Western-reachable extortion plumbing
Exposure: contact runs through onionmail.org addresses and the leak site courts journalists by name on its contact form. The crew's pressure model depends on Western media attention, which is an input it does not control. The Berlin case shows it will publish in full when the victim refuses, which removes the auction's leverage on the next victim who reads the record.
Evidence: Sophos on the contact form [34]; onionmail pattern [1]; Berlin publication [37][38].
Indicator: turnover of contact addresses in ransom notes; press pickup rate of new listings; victim refusal rate over successive high-profile cases.
Confidence: CREDIBLE.
