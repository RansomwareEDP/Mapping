Monthly Cybercrime Ecosystem Intelligence Report

Coverage period 3 October to 1 November 2026. The UK designated the Kyrgyz payment rails beside Grinex and A7, the FBI and Dutch police took down the Virtualine bulletproof host and its customer records, a Russian Qilin member reached German custody, North Korea took $6.3 billion from Binance, and leak-site claims rose on a debut brand.

SECTION 1 - EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

  1. The UK sanctioned the crypto rails that sit next to Grinex and A7. On 8 October it designated the operator of the Cryptomus and Heleket payment processors, the Kyrgyz exchange TokenSpot and two Kyrgyz payment processors. Blockchain analysts say TokenSpot alone moved more than $950 million to Grinex, Garantex and A7. Confidence: CONFIRMED for the designations, CREDIBLE REPORTING for the flow figure.
  2. North Korean state hackers took about $6.3 billion from Binance in 20 minutes on 29 October, the largest crypto theft on record, and the FBI named them two days later. Binance is the venue that matters most for ransomware cash-out, and stablecoin issuers froze about $2.4 billion of the proceeds within hours. Confidence: CONFIRMED for the theft and attribution, CREDIBLE REPORTING for the figures.
  3. A Russian national described as a core Qilin member is in German custody after Japan handed him over. Japan confirmed it on 8 October. Qilin still posted about 140 victims in October, so no operational effect is visible. Confidence: CONFIRMED for the arrest and transfer; the transfer date is disputed.
  4. The FBI seized the Virtualine bulletproof hosting domain on 27 October. Dutch police took dozens of physical servers in Limburg the same day, knocked hundreds of virtual servers offline and now hold data on more than 3,000 customers. Virtualine took more illicit crypto in 2026 than any other bulletproof host one analytics firm tracks, though ransomware groups were only about 5 percent of its direct payers. Confidence: CONFIRMED for the seizures and the customer data, CREDIBLE REPORTING for the flow figures.
  5. Leak-site claims rose about 26 percent to roughly 1,127. A September debut brand, N0n, took first place with 154 claims, ahead of The Gentlemen and Qilin. Confidence: CREDIBLE REPORTING (single tracker; others put the month higher).

  • N0n debuted on 18 September and led October. On 12 October it listed 38 port and logistics firms with a seven-day deadline. No victim had confirmed a breach by 29 October. A batch that size with no confirmations points to recycled or inflated claims. Confidence: CREDIBLE REPORTING for the counts, ANALYST INFERENCE for the claim quality.
  • Qilin is the month's key enforcement story. A 28-year-old Russian was held in Osaka on a German warrant and is now in German custody. German investigators say they had been inside Qilin's systems for months. No infrastructure was seized and the brand kept posting. Confidence: CONFIRMED.
  • SafePay received a US advisory written with German and UK police. It says the group has hit at least 600 organisations since August 2024. Confidence: CONFIRMED.
  • INC and Lynx are named as customers of a FortiGate access-selling campaign and, with Qilin, as users of a PAN-OS flaw. Confidence: CONFIRMED.

  • Rhysida is in a posting surge. It listed 17 victims in October against 7 in August, concentrated on US local government, legal services and German public bodies. No enforcement action has ever named the group or the crew behind it. Confidence: CONFIRMED for the counts.
  • ShinyHunters has not posted since its site went offline on 30 September. A 16-year-old was arrested in Jordan and the FBI reported a further arrest on 9 October. Confidence: CREDIBLE REPORTING.
  • A hospital ransomware event hit the University of New Mexico health system on 26 October. Ambulances were diverted. A new brand, BYOD, claims it. Confidence: CONFIRMED for the disruption, claim unverified.

Data extortion trend

  • Data was stolen in 71 percent of ransomware cases in Q3, and nearly 80 percent of theft-only cases ended without payment. Confidence: CREDIBLE REPORTING (one incident responder's client base).
  • One vendor puts the Q3 payment rate just under 21 percent, down from 50 percent. Payment sizes rose: the median was $400,000.
  • Fewer victims pay, but those who pay pay more. Groups are hitting larger firms and pressing them harder. Confidence: ANALYST INFERENCE.

  • The Kyrgyz payment layer is now designated on two fronts. The US is moving against A7 Sub-Agents and the UK has listed TokenSpot and two Kyrgyz processors. Confidence: CONFIRMED.
  • Cryptomus and Heleket took money from more than 15,000 illicit actors, including Black Basta proceeds. They also took payments for Zservers, Stark Industries and Aeza hosting. The listing ties ransomware cash-out to bulletproof hosting payments. Confidence: CREDIBLE REPORTING.
  • A7A5 transfers collapsed from about 600 a day to under 20 after strikes on Russian data centres on 7 to 8 October. A7 then opened a service that pays suppliers by bank wire. The cause is physical, not sanctions. Confidence: CREDIBLE REPORTING (single analytics source).
  • A US ransom recovery firm was charged with secretly paying more than $8 million in ransoms while billing clients $19 million. Confidence: CREDIBLE REPORTING.
  • Binance lost about $6.3 billion in 20 minutes on 29 October after an engineer's laptop was compromised. Tether, Circle and others froze about $2.4 billion the same day, and Binance halted withdrawals for days while it rebuilt the service and covered the loss from its own funds. The thieves are North Korean, not Russian, but the venue is the one where ransomware proceeds also end up, and the freezes show how fast stablecoin issuers can move when asked. Confidence: CONFIRMED for the theft and attribution, CREDIBLE REPORTING for the figures.

Infrastructure hosting

  • Aeza has grown to more than 137,000 IPv4 addresses in 536 ranges, spread across 11 legal entities. Sanctions did not shrink it. Confidence: CREDIBLE REPORTING.
  • Stark Industries' successor, THE.Hosting, withdrew nearly all its address space in early October and now routes through other networks. Confidence: CREDIBLE REPORTING (single source).
  • Virtualine ran on Omegatech, which took over from Railnet in February. After the seizure its ranges stayed partly reachable. Its own advertising describes the escape route: from March 2026 it sold a protection plan that moved a customer's infrastructure to an unrelated shell company after a law-enforcement request, then switched the server back on. Expect the customers to be found on new names, not new hardware. Confidence: CONFIRMED for the plan, ANALYST INFERENCE for the expectation.

Cascade failure analysis

  • Kyrgyz layer. If the processors lose correspondent banking, A7A5 and Grinex lose their main bank exit. The October data-centre strikes showed what happens: transfers fell by more than 95 percent within a week, and A7 moved to bank wires. That move puts the traffic back into the banking system where the rule applies.
  • Cryptomus and Heleket. If exchanges block their deposit addresses, bulletproof hosts lose an easy way to get paid and ransomware actors lose a cash-out. One exchange deposit address took more than $308 million from these flows. That address is the choke point.
  • Bulletproof hosting. Seizing a domain does not stop the network. Customer records do. Dutch police hold data on more than 3,000 Virtualine customers and say they will pursue the ones who used the servers for crime. Matching that data to stealer and ransomware infrastructure gives a second wave of targets, and the shell-company migration plan tells investigators where to look: the same customers under a new company name.
  • Large exchanges. The Binance theft shows the other face of the chokepoint. One compromised laptop moved $6.3 billion out of the venue where ransomware proceeds also land, and withdrawals stayed closed for days. The freeze tools that caught $2.4 billion of North Korean money in hours are the same tools available against ransomware cash-out when issuers are asked in time. Confidence: ANALYST INFERENCE.

Structural vulnerability summary

The ecosystem depends on a few shared payment rails and a few hosts that reappear under new names. Brands are cheap to replace; rails, customer lists and skilled stealer developers are not. Pressure on the rails rose sharply in October.
